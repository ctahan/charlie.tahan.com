On November 16, 2026, I gave the opening keynote of the Quantum–ISSH Symposium at the University of Wisconsin–Madison: “Does Quantum Matter in the Age of AI?” ISSH stands for the Intersections of Social Sciences and Humanities with Quantum. The symposium brought quantum scientists and engineers together with social scientists and humanists to start a new line of work at UW on quantum and society.

For me it was a homecoming. I did my PhD in physics at Madison, and in 2005, my last year there, I co-taught an undergraduate course called Nanotechnology and Society. It asked, “What are the consequences for our society?” Twenty years later I came back to ask that question about quantum and AI.

My core argument is about the delta between AI and quantum: what quantum computers can do that AI on ordinary computers can’t. If the delta is small, AI captures most of what matters, and quantum adds a little. If it is big, quantum computers become telescopes into nature, producing data about molecules and materials that nothing else can, and AI trained on that data could take a step we can’t predict, the way training on code made language models better at more than code. I’ve argued both. My bet is that it’s huge. AI is the story. Quantum is how the rest of the universe gets into it.

The talk was also an experiment. I made it with AI agents, the Automatons, who share the byline. They dug up my old work, drafted with me, objected to my arguments on stage, and wrote the app the talk ran on. Behind every slide, a simulated quantum wave grew from the ideas I raised, and the app listened as I spoke. Twice the talk branched: once by a show of hands, and once by the answers of twenty-four AI models.

## Highlights

- Quantum policy stayed narrow on purpose: it focused on what is fundamentally different (security, economy, workforce) so it wouldn’t become a grab-all. ([7:57](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jH9mP4x4xgg&t=477))
- Nano is the precedent. It didn’t vanish; it dissolved into semiconductors and materials, and AI will absorb technology the same way. ([14:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jH9mP4x4xgg&t=878))
- Being a scientist is changing this year. AI speeds up every step, but it is still slop; the game is verifying it, and the slop is getting harder to catch. ([21:30](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jH9mP4x4xgg&t=1290))
- Small teams with AI move very fast if they know what they are doing, so the bottleneck moves to hardware and hands-on training. ([25:24](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jH9mP4x4xgg&t=1524))
- Quantum is “what is left when you take AI and you subtract the rest of the universe.” Big and strange aren’t enough; it has to be useful, and only a few algorithms are. ([31:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jH9mP4x4xgg&t=1898))
- The bet: quantum computers as telescopes that feed AI quantum data, as code made LLMs better at everything. It loses if classical methods can supply that data. ([38:38](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jH9mP4x4xgg&t=2318))
- When AI makes everything, nobody knows how things are made. Most people won’t care; the open question is the model directing it. ([42:53](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jH9mP4x4xgg&t=2573))
- The charge: find where quantum really differs from AI, and bring the public in, because the public will conflate the two. ([43:59](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jH9mP4x4xgg&t=2639))
- Don’t fear-monger. AI is the real story, but we don’t know the step function quantum will bring to it. And we don’t know what will happen when you include the rest of the universe. ([45:41](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jH9mP4x4xgg&t=2741))
